Why these boreholes?
The number and depth of boreholes were determined using the Philippe et al. (2010) ASHRAE three-pulse method, which accounts for short-term (1-hour), monthly, and annual heat pulses into the ground. Soil thermal conductivity was predicted from USGS geological data. Building loads were derived from DOE EnergyPlus prototype models scaled to your building type and climate zone.
+
Heating vs cooling?
The borefield is sized for the governing design condition. If heating dominates, the borefield is sized to extract heat from the ground in winter. If cooling dominates, it rejects heat in summer. The non-governing season is also served by the same borefield, but at a lower load. Thermal imbalance occurs when one season extracts significantly more heat than the other injects, which can cause long-term ground temperature drift.

How does cost compare?
GSHP systems typically have higher upfront installation costs than conventional HVAC but lower operating costs due to higher efficiency. The conventional benchmark shown is for the heating and cooling plant only (excluding interior air distribution). The GSHP cost includes borefield drilling and heat pump equipment. Operating cost estimates assume US EIA average commercial energy rates.

What is the peaker?
A hybrid system adds a small supplemental heating or cooling unit (the peaker) to handle only the top peak loads, typically the highest 5 to 15 percent of hours per year. By sizing the borefield for the majority of loads rather than the absolute peak, you reduce drilling length and upfront cost while maintaining high efficiency for most operating hours. The GSHP handles the base load; the peaker handles the extreme peaks.

Is the soil good?
Higher thermal conductivity means heat moves through the ground more easily, reducing the total borehole length needed. The conductivity value used here was predicted from USGS geological data using machine learning models. For high-value projects, an in-situ Thermal Response Test can confirm this value and may allow a less conservative final design. Higher ground temperature also affects heat pump efficiency.
